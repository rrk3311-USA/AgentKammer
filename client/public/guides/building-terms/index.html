The Grammar of a Building

A building tells you how it was made, if you know how to listen.

Thirty terms, each drawn plainly, for reading a Manhattan building from the sidewalk to the radiator. Tap any plate for the full entry. I'd keep it open on your phone at the next open house.

The Face

Facade and exterior

The street side of a building is its handshake. It tells you when it was built, how it was built and, with a little squinting, how it has been kept.

A curious thing

Plenty of the “stone” cornices on old New York buildings are galvanized sheet metal, ordered from trade catalogs and painted to pass for limestone. From the sidewalk, the disguise still works.

Why it matters when you buy

A cornice is lovely and heavy, and it hangs over the sidewalk. Buildings taller than six stories are inspected under the city's facade program every five years, so its condition can turn up in the building's repair budget.

A curious thing

The lintels at Stonehenge are locked to their uprights with mortise and tenon joints, a trick borrowed from carpentry. Some 4,500 years on, the woodworking still holds.

Why it matters when you buy

Cracks fanning out from the corner of a window are often a rusting steel lintel expanding inside the wall. It's a routine repair, though not a cheap one when it runs across a whole facade.

A curious thing

The word is Dutch, from stoep, but the height is pure New York. One well documented reason stoops climbed so high was the street below, which in the horse era was ankle deep in manure.

Why it matters when you buy

The parlor floor at the top of the stoop usually has the tallest ceilings and the best windows in the house. The stoop itself is masonry outdoors in a New York winter, and it ages accordingly.

A curious thing

The wedding cake skyline owes a lot to one building. The Equitable Building at 120 Broadway, finished in 1915, threw a shadow of about seven acres, and the 1916 zoning rules followed.

Why it matters when you buy

A setback is often where a private terrace comes from. Ask who maintains the roofing underneath it, because the answer varies by building and by document.

A curious thing

The Romans were doing this two thousand years ago. The Colosseum wears half columns on its first three levels, then switches to flat Corinthian pilasters on the top.

Why it matters when you buy

Pilasters are a tell. They suggest a building that spent money on its face, which says something, if not everything, about how it was built.

The Bones

Structure

You never see most of this once the paint goes on. You feel it, though, in where the walls can move and how the floor answers your step.

A curious thing

Chicago's Monadnock Building, finished in the early 1890s, stands on brick walls about six feet thick at the base. It's the best argument for steel framing ever made in masonry.

Why it matters when you buy

The open kitchen in your head depends on this one. Removing or opening a bearing wall involves an engineer, permits and, in a co-op or condo, board approval, and sometimes the answer is simply no.

Related terms

  • Beam
  • Column
  • Slab

A curious thing

The most famous columns in Athens are women. Six sculpted maidens, the caryatids, hold up a porch of the Erechtheion, and five of the originals now rest indoors at the Acropolis Museum.

Why it matters when you buy

In a column-framed building, walls between them can often move, which is good news for renovation. A column in the middle of a living room cannot, and it rarely appears on the floor plan as prominently as it does in person.

Related terms

  • Load-bearing wall

A curious thing

Peter Cooper rolled wrought iron I-beams at his own Trenton ironworks and put them in the Cooper Union Foundation Building, finished in 1859. Few founders can say they made the bones of their own building.

Why it matters when you buy

Where a beam drops below the ceiling it steals height in that strip of the room. Worth looking up during a showing, literally, before you picture the tall bookcase.

Related terms

  • Column
  • Lintel
  • Slab

A curious thing

The beamless concrete floor started in Minneapolis. C.A.P. Turner set the slabs of his 1906 Johnson-Bovey Building on flared “mushroom” columns, an idea that has been quietly holding up buildings ever since.

Why it matters when you buy

The slab is shared structure. Drilling through it to move a drain or add a pipe usually needs the building's blessing and an engineer's review.

Related terms

  • Riser
  • Steel frame vs. concrete frame
  • Beam

A curious thing

The world's first reinforced concrete skyscraper went up in Cincinnati, not New York. The 16-story Ingalls Building was finished in 1903 and is still standing.

Why it matters when you buy

Each has a personality. Concrete flat slabs tend to mean lower ceilings with few beams. Steel with masonry tends to mean thick walls and plaster. Neither is better in the abstract. It depends on how you live.

The Rooms

Interior detail

Trim and proportion are where an apartment shows its manners. Some of it is original. Some of it arrived last spring from a catalog.

A curious thing

Crown molding is a cornice that moved indoors. Outside, the classical cornice was built to throw rain off a wall. Inside, its only job is to make the ceiling look expensive.

Why it matters when you buy

Original plaster crown is hard to fake and expensive to replace. When it survives intact, it suggests a renovation history that was respectful, which tends to be good news elsewhere too.

Related terms

  • Wainscoting
  • Coffered ceiling
  • Prewar co-op

A curious thing

Wainscot first named the wood, not the paneling. It meant a fine, straight-grained oak shipped from the Baltic, from the Middle Dutch wagenschot.

Why it matters when you buy

Real wood paneling adds warmth and some resale charm. Applied trim imitating it adds charm too, just less of the warmth. Tap it gently and you'll usually know.

Related terms

  • Crown molding
  • Pocket doors
  • Enfilade

A curious thing

The Pantheon's dome has 140 coffers in five rings, and they aren't only for show. Each one takes out concrete, lightening a dome that has stood with no reinforcing steel for nearly 1,900 years.

Why it matters when you buy

Coffers need height to look right. Under a low ceiling they press down, so judge them by how the room feels, not by the photograph.

Related terms

  • Crown molding
  • Beam
  • Enfilade

A curious thing

The enfilade is a palace idea. At Versailles the king's state rooms run in a single line, and how far down it you were allowed to walk told everyone exactly where you stood.

Why it matters when you buy

An enfilade makes an apartment feel larger and more formal than its square footage. It also means some rooms are passed through, a small thing until you want to close a door.

Related terms

  • Pocket doors
  • Coffered ceiling
  • Prewar co-op

A curious thing

Pocket doors were a Victorian enthusiasm, already common by the 1850s. The early ones rolled on floor tracks. Overhead hangers came later in the century, which is why the good ones still glide.

Why it matters when you buy

Lovely between a living room and a library. If a vintage pocket door sticks, the repair often means opening the wall, so give them a slow test slide.

The Families

Prewar, postwar and building types

Manhattan buildings come in recognizable families, each with its own habits. Knowing the family is half of knowing the apartment.

A curious thing

Much of New York's brownstone came from quarries in Portland, Connecticut. They flooded in the 1930s and are now a park, where people swim and scuba dive in the hole the city's facades came out of.

Why it matters when you buy

The stone is beautiful and soft, and it flakes as it weathers. Facade restoration is a real line item, and in a historic district the Landmarks Preservation Commission will have opinions about the work.

Related terms

  • Stoop
  • Cornice
  • Lintel

A curious thing

The Manhattan co-op is older than most people guess. The Rembrandt on West 57th Street, finished in 1881, is widely called the city's first, one of architect Philip Hubert's “Home Clubs.”

Why it matters when you buy

Prewar suggests thick walls, plaster and generous rooms. Co-op means a board reviews buyers and renovation plans. Both facts shape the apartment as much as the floor plan does.

Related terms

  • Crown molding
  • Steam radiator
  • Postwar white brick

A curious thing

The white brick look has a birthday. Manhattan House on East 66th Street, finished in 1951, is widely credited as the first, and it has been a city landmark since 2007.

Why it matters when you buy

Often the most square footage for the money, with efficient layouts and lower ceilings. The trade is a little character for a lot of practicality, which some people find a bargain.

Related terms

  • Prewar co-op
  • Steel frame vs. concrete frame
  • Through-wall AC sleeve

A curious thing

Lever House on Park Avenue, finished in 1952, made the glass tower respectable in New York. It was landmarked in 1982, barely thirty years old.

Why it matters when you buy

Floor-to-ceiling glass is a view and a thermostat problem at once. Afternoon sun can heat a room hard, so I'd be inclined to visit at the hour you would actually be home.

The Plumbing of Comfort

Systems

Heat, cooling, water and laundry. Unglamorous, permanent, and the part of a home you notice every single day.

A curious thing

Modern air conditioning was born in Brooklyn, and not for people. Willis Carrier's 1902 system kept the paper at the Sackett-Wilhelms printing plant from swelling in the humidity. Comfort came later.

Why it matters when you buy

An existing sleeve is quietly valuable. Cutting a new one through a facade usually needs board approval, and often more paperwork if the building is landmarked.

Related terms

  • PTAC unit
  • Postwar white brick
  • Steam radiator

A curious thing

The PTAC's ancestor was a 1935 under-window cooling unit made by Chrysler. Yes, the car company.

Why it matters when you buy

PTACs give each room its own control, and each owner its own electric bill. They can also be noisier than central systems, so turn one on during a visit.

Related terms

  • Through-wall AC sleeve
  • Glass tower
  • Steam radiator

A curious thing

The water in your riser comes from upstate reservoirs through enormous tunnels. City Water Tunnel No. 3 has been under construction since 1970, which puts most kitchen renovations in perspective.

Why it matters when you buy

Kitchens and baths cluster near risers. Many buildings won't let a wet room move over a neighbor's dry one, which quietly limits how far a renovation can wander.

Related terms

  • Slab
  • Washer and dryer venting
  • Steam radiator

A curious thing

The first modern electric dryer, Hamilton's “June Day,” went on sale in 1938, invented by a North Dakotan named J. Ross Moore. Co-op boards have had opinions about it ever since.

Why it matters when you buy

In-unit laundry is rarely just a matter of buying machines. Many co-ops restrict or prohibit it, and where it's allowed, venting and the board's rules decide what you can install.

Related terms

  • Riser
  • Through-wall AC sleeve
  • Prewar co-op

A curious thing

Some Manhattan buildings have no boiler at all. They buy their steam from Con Edison, which runs the largest district steam system in the country through about 105 miles of pipe under the streets.

Why it matters when you buy

You inherit the building's heat, and you don't control it much. City rules require heat from October through May, and old steam systems can run a little enthusiastic. Those clanks are part of the bargain.

Coast to Coast

Only in New York, Only in California

Some things you only meet on one coast. A cedar tank on a Tribeca roof, a bungalow in Pasadena. Each is local knowledge, and each tends to surface in the paperwork sooner or later.

A curious thing

Many are still wood, built by a few family firms that date to the 1890s, Rosenwach and Isseks Brothers among them. A Rosenwach tank is held together by steel hoops alone, with no glue.

Why it matters when you buy

The tank is the building's plumbing in plain sight. City rules require it to be inspected and cleaned every year, and replacing one eventually lands in the building's budget. A charming silhouette, and a line item.

Related terms

  • Riser
  • Prewar co-op
  • Cornice

A curious thing

New York's fire escapes trace back to a February 1860 tenement fire on Elm Street that killed ten women and children. The city's first law requiring exterior escapes passed that April.

Why it matters when you buy

It looks like a balcony and isn't one. The law expects it to stay clear, so no planters or chairs, and any window gate onto it has to be an approved type. Count it as a view of the street, not square footage.

Related terms

  • Lintel
  • Cornice
  • Prewar co-op

A curious thing

The 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake made the term famous in San Francisco's Marina District, where buildings with garages tucked into the ground floor racked and buckled.

Why it matters when you buy

Some California cities, Los Angeles and San Francisco among them, have required many of these buildings to be retrofitted. I'd want to know whether the work is done, permitted and paid for, since that answer shapes both safety and the building's finances.

Related terms

  • Load-bearing wall
  • Steel frame vs. concrete frame

A curious thing

Santa Barbara looks the way it does partly because of an earthquake. After the 1925 quake the city rebuilt with a design review board steering it toward Spanish Colonial Revival, and the look stuck.

Why it matters when you buy

Original tile, ironwork and plaster are the point, and they are hard to replace in kind. Clay roofs last a long time but tiles crack when walked on, and stucco shows every settlement. Look closely, and with affection.

Related terms

  • Craftsman bungalow
  • ADU (accessory dwelling unit)
  • Pilaster

A curious thing

You could once order a house by mail. Sears sold kit homes from 1908 to 1940, an estimated 70,000 or more, bungalows prominent among them.

Why it matters when you buy

The built-ins, wood trim and old-growth lumber are the inheritance. So is a century of wiring, plumbing and foundation work, done well or otherwise. An older California house can also call for seismic work, so it's worth asking what has been done.

Related terms

  • Spanish Colonial Revival
  • ADU (accessory dwelling unit)
  • Wainscoting

A curious thing

California's 2017 reforms set off a quiet boom in backyards. Los Angeles went from 80 ADU permits in 2016 to more than 7,000 in 2022.

Why it matters when you buy

An ADU can house a parent, an office or a tenant, and that flexibility has real value. What matters most is whether it was permitted. A permitted ADU is part of the property. An unpermitted one can be a question mark.

Related terms

Learn the words and the walk-through changes. You stop asking if you like a place and start noticing what it's made of, which tends to be the better question.
